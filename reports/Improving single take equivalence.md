# Fix Calibration and Shoulders Before Statistics

The fastest route to more equivalent angles is to measure better, not to test differently. Four changes fit that rule, in this order. First, replace the board-free self-calibration with **measured phone intrinsics plus a printed-board extrinsic calibration**. Second, give the shoulder the **same point semantics in suit and video**, and record a short **calibration block** (rest, arms raised, slow elbow bend, wrist sweep) before every sign. Third, put more **pixels on the hands**: both phones at 1080p and 60 fps, a fast shutter, tight framing, and a dedicated hand-keypoint stage. Fourth, upgrade synchronisation from a good point estimate to a **verified, uncertainty-carrying estimate**: real frame timestamps, continuous sub-frame refinement, an impulsive arm event before and after the sign, and lag draws inside the Monte Carlo. The evidence says these are the real bottlenecks. In After, changing only the hand definition moved the self-calibrated camera baseline from 56.1° to 64.8°, and the right-elbow upper bound moved from 8.0° to 14.4° with it. In Action, testing stopped because video read left shoulder elevation 20–24° lower than the suit for 42% of the sign, a sustained, posture-dependent gap that sync cannot cause and a single rest-hold offset cannot remove ([sequential testing report](Single%20take%20equivalence%20sequential%20testing.md)). Two statistical changes are legitimate but secondary. One is a graphical or fallback procedure, which keeps the trunk-first priority without letting one doubtful angle zero the alpha of every later one. The other is multi-take replication, which any claim beyond "this take" needs. Every change below must be committed to `equivalence_margins.json` before the next take is analysed. Applied to After and Action, all of them are exploratory. The ±15° RMS margin stays as it is, and a pass still means agreement with the two-phone video for that take only.

## Self-calibration is now the largest single source of instability

Every validated low-cost markerless system calibrates against a known target, not against the body being measured. OpenCap reaches a **mean joint-angle error of 4.5°** with two iPhones. It uses intrinsics stored per phone model and extrinsics from one image of a checkerboard printed on A4, and printed boards performed about as well as precision boards ([Uhlrich et al. 2023](https://journals.plos.org/ploscompbiol/article?id=10.1371/journal.pcbi.1011462)). Pose2Sim targets intrinsic reprojection error at or below 0.5 px and extrinsic error below 1 cm, and recommends recalibrating after any zoom change ([Pose2Sim documentation](https://perfanalytics.github.io/pose2sim/)). Once calibration is that good, its contribution to joint-angle error is small: a deliberate 1 cm calibration error changed walking angles by only **0.19°** ([Pagnon et al. 2021](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8512754/)). Calibration from body keypoints alone does much worse. CasCalib averaged about **11% focal error** and **0.8–10.7° pairwise rotation error** on real data, even with synchronised cameras. Its authors skipped bundle adjustment in the two-view case because two views give "insufficient constraints under noisy keypoints" ([CasCalib](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.06845v1)).

SignSure shows exactly that failure mode. The pipeline has seen focal estimates driven to about 2.1× nominal and reprojection errors of 4.6–7.3 px ([calibration notes](../research_notes/Improving%20single%20take%20equivalence/camera_calibration_setup.md)). Because wrist-to-hand bone lengths enter the bone-length-constancy term, a new hand point moved the After camera solution by almost 9°. The mechanism explains why this matters. In a two-view orthographic model, reconstructing with the wrong convergence angle applies a scale plus a shear in the depth plane. For a 56° → 65° error, a first-order calculation gives a worst-case change of **about 10°** in an in-plane joint angle of 60–120°. The same calculation gives at most 2.3° for a 2° error, and a 10% depth-scale error alone gives up to 5.5° ([calibration notes, computed](../research_notes/Improving%20single%20take%20equivalence/camera_calibration_setup.md)). With a pass requiring a true suit–video RMS of about 9° or less at 3 px noise ([15° report](Single%20take%20equivalence%20at%2015%20degrees.md)), a calibration that can move an angle by 5–10° is spending most of the budget before the suit is even compared.

The fix costs one printed sheet. Measure each phone's intrinsics once per resolution and lens: film a printed chessboard (at least 20 corners) sweeping across the whole frame, target ≤ 0.5 px, and use the main 1× lens only. Lock focus and exposure, turn off digital stabilisation (it crops and warps every frame, which invalidates a fixed intrinsic model), and record in the same resolution and orientation that was calibrated. For each take, film an A4 or A3 board held at the signer's chest position, seen by both phones, ideally in several poses. Distance from the board raises extrinsic error, so the board belongs where the hands will be ([Uhlrich et al. 2023](https://journals.plos.org/ploscompbiol/article?id=10.1371/journal.pcbi.1011462)). Pose2Sim's alternative, ten or more tape-measured scene landmarks, needs no printing and works on unsynchronised cameras ([Pose2Sim documentation](https://perfanalytics.github.io/pose2sim/)). Keep the keypoint self-calibration, but demote it. Use it only to refine extrinsics, with intrinsics fixed and the board solution as the starting point. Report the board-versus-keypoint difference as a calibration diagnostic. In the Monte Carlo, sample the board calibration's own (much smaller) uncertainty instead of refitting focal length. A bone definition can then no longer move the cameras.

Camera geometry is the second lever. For two views, depth noise relative to image noise scales as √(1 + cos²θ)/sin θ. That is **1.38× at 56°, 1.20× at 65° and 1.0× at 90°**, and calibration rotation is also better conditioned at wide angles ([calibration notes, computed](../research_notes/Improving%20single%20take%20equivalence/camera_calibration_setup.md)). Qualisys asks for more than 60° and never less than 30° ([Qualisys](https://docs.qualisys.com/qtm/content/system_setup/camera_positioning.htm)). OpenCap's main results used about 90° between cameras ([Uhlrich et al. 2023](https://journals.plos.org/ploscompbiol/article?id=10.1371/journal.pcbi.1011462)). Set the second phone 70–90° from the frontal one, and no more than about 110°, beyond which near-opposite views invite left/right swaps. A third phone on the other side gave OpenCap less than 0.3° of median kinematic gain ([Uhlrich et al. 2023](https://journals.plos.org/ploscompbiol/article?id=10.1371/journal.pcbi.1011462)). Signing is different, though: the hands cross the body and one side view loses the far hand. Joint-angle confidence intervals widen sharply at occluded joints and times when cameras are few ([arXiv 2502.06486](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.06486)). A third phone is therefore worth adding once the first two are calibrated properly, mainly to shrink tail errors and the Monte Carlo spread on the far wrist.

## Shoulder elevation fails on a definition gap that grows as the arm rises

The shoulder-elevation disagreement is not noise. In After, video reads the right shoulder 13–15° low while the arm is raised. In Action, it reads the left shoulder 20–24° low for 42% of the sign, and the replicate check fails ([sequential testing report](Single%20take%20equivalence%20sequential%20testing.md)). The gap is near zero at rest, which is exactly why the pre-sign-hold offset cannot remove it. Two kinds of evidence point to a definition mismatch rather than suit error. First, markerless shoulder centres are not glenohumeral centres: OpenPose, AlphaPose and DeepLabCut shoulder points sit **24–34 mm** from the marker-based centre ([Needham et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8526586/)), and glenohumeral centre estimation degrades above about 60° of elevation ([DU faculty paper](https://digitalcommons.du.edu/mat_mech_eng_faculty/35)). Second, the two systems err in different ways. In an elbow study with both, IMU deviations were mostly **constant**, while pose-estimation deviations were **angle-dependent** and underestimated large flexion ([ETH Zurich 2025](https://www.research-collection.ethz.ch/items/62b944dd-2f46-401c-acdc-b9d98ee4d441)).

The most plausible mechanism is anatomical. The COCO shoulder keypoint rides on the visible shoulder contour, which rises and shifts medially as the clavicle and scapula elevate. A 3 cm rise at the proximal end of a 30 cm humerus tilts the shoulder-to-elbow vector by about 5–6° toward horizontal. The raised shoulder keypoint also tilts the video trunk axis, which is built from the mid-shoulder point, toward the raised arm. Both effects reduce video elevation. The suit's upper-arm direction comes from the upper-arm IMU and its trunk from the back sensors, so a shrug barely changes either ([suit alignment notes](../research_notes/Improving%20single%20take%20equivalence/suit_accuracy_alignment.md)). This is a hypothesis, and the steelman against it deserves stating. Theia3D shoulder angles were reported mostly *higher* than marker-based angles ([PMC12416023](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12416023/)), and no study reports a signed bias of markerless humerothoracic elevation as a function of elevation. The direction is therefore not settled by the literature. It is consistent with the pitching study, where shoulder abduction was the one metric clearly out of tolerance (MAE 6.2° and 21.4°) because the joint centre hides under soft tissue ([arXiv 2603.04864](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.04864)).

The fix has two layers, and definitions come first. Segment-frame definition is the largest single source of IMU joint-angle error: **11.3–18.7° RMSD**, against 3.8–9.1° for soft-tissue artefact ([Kamstra et al. 2022](https://research.tudelft.nl/files/142948797/sensors_22_09765.pdf)). Model error also exceeded sensor error significantly in Xsens ([Robert-Lachaîne et al. 2017](https://pharesst.irsst.qc.ca/etudes-primaires/139)). Give both systems the same shoulder point. One way is to take the FBX point that corresponds to the COCO label (the acromial end of the Mixamo clavicle bone) instead of the `Arm` bone head. The other is to shift the video point inferomedially by a per-person vector measured in the still hold. Then build the trunk axis in both systems from points that do not move with the shoulder girdle, such as the hip midpoint to a thorax point carried rigidly from the rest hold. This mirrors what the knuckle centre did for the hand.

The second layer is a **calibration block** recorded before every sign: the existing still hold, a 1–2 s hold with each arm at about 90° abduction and 90° flexion, a slow elbow bend, and a slow wrist flexion and deviation sweep. It gives each angle at least two levels, which is enough to tell a constant offset from a gain. Fit offset and gain per angle on the calibration block only, never on the sign window. Use Deming (orthogonal) regression with the error-variance ratio taken from the existing still-frame noise model, because ordinary least squares attenuates the slope when both systems have error ([Türkiye Klinikleri methods paper](https://turkiyeklinikleri.com/article/en-iki-olcum-tekniginin-uyumunu-incelemede-kullanilan-grafiksel-ve-regresyon-yontemleri-uygulamali-ve-senaryolara-dayali-bir-calisma-64364.html)). Re-estimate both inside every Monte Carlo draw, as the offset already is. A stroke-rehabilitation preprint recommends exactly this kind of angle-dependent correction, validated on independent data ([Research Square preprint](https://www.researchsquare.com/article/rs-11268533/v1)). The risk is over-correction, since a gain can absorb real suit error at high angles. Pre-register a cap on the gain (for example 0.8–1.25), report raw, offset-only and offset-plus-gain RMS side by side, and word the claim as "agreement after a pre-registered definition alignment".

The suit side needs matching discipline. Calibrate the Rokoko straight pose immediately before each take, at the performance spot, away from metal. Confirm that no sensor shows yellow and that the field fluctuation stays within about ±0.15 G ([Rokoko support](https://support.rokoko.com/hc/en-us/articles/44474445861521-Degaussing-your-space-A-guide-to-magnetic-interference)). Keep takes short: Rokoko itself says Sensor Fusion 2.0 reduces magnetic drift but does not eliminate it ([Rokoko insights](https://rokoko.com/insights/sensor-fusion-2-0-more-accuracy-less-drift)). Constant biases of this size are normal for IMU suits. Perception Neuron showed **elbow biases of −12 to −21° in every task** alongside 1.9–3.9° curve RMSE ([Choo et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8782534/)). That supports treating SignSure's offsets as definitional, provided the post-sign hold gives the same offset as the pre-sign hold. A difference between the two holds is drift, and the protocol should flag it.

## Wrists need pixels on the hand more than a new model

The wrist is weakest for a reason that arithmetic makes plain. Pointing-direction error is about atan(σ⊥/L). With roughly 5 px of perpendicular noise on a wrist-to-knuckle segment about 20 px long, as at 480p, that is **about 14°**, the same order as the observed 10–21° wrist Monte Carlo SD ([keypoint notes](../research_notes/Improving%20single%20take%20equivalence/video_keypoint_precision.md)). Halving the noise or doubling the segment's pixel length halves the angle noise. Swapping models within the RTMW family will not do that. The pipeline's `rtmw-dw-x-l` checkpoint is RTMW-l ([MMPose README](https://github.com/open-mmlab/mmpose/blob/main/projects/rtmpose/README.md)), and RTMW-x scores 66.4 hand AP on COCO-WholeBody against **66.3** for RTMW-l. Resolution matters far more: raising model input from 256×192 to 384×288 adds **6.5 hand AP**, the largest gain of any body part. Hands remain the weakest region in every model, behind body (76.3) and face (88.4), and the authors attribute this to how few input pixels a hand gets ([RTMW paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.08634v1)).

The cheapest gain is therefore in the recording. Record both phones at 1080p and 60 fps, and frame from hips to above the head so each hand spans at least 80–100 native pixels. Use optical framing, not digital zoom. Use a shutter of 1/500–1/1000 s under bright light: at 1–2 m/s a 1/30 s exposure smears the hand by 3–7 cm, about the length of the wrist-to-knuckle segment. Biomechanics groups make the same choice; one Theia3D study reduced shutter time "as much as possible" to limit blur ([Song et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10494994)). Pose2Sim found little loss going from 1080p to 720p for whole-body framing but warns that accuracy drops below 60 Hz for fast motion ([Pose2Sim documentation](https://perfanalytics.github.io/pose2sim/)). Nothing below 720p was validated, which is the Action side view's situation. Place the side phone so it sees the back of the hand rather than its edge, because foreshortened knuckles inflate angular noise.

The software upgrade is a second-stage hand model on a crop of the full-resolution frame. Crop each hand at 1.5–2× hand size around the RTMW wrist and hand points. Then run RTMPose-m hand (PCK@0.2 of 96.4 on the Hand5 benchmark, 2.6 GFLOPs, CPU-friendly; [MMPose README](https://github.com/open-mmlab/mmpose/blob/main/projects/rtmpose/README.md)) or a structurally different model such as WiLoR ([CVPR 2025](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/CVPR2025/papers/Potamias_WiLoR_End-to-end_3D_Hand_Localization_and_Reconstruction_in-the-wild_CVPR_2025_paper.pdf)) or HaMeR (PCK@0.1 of 71–78 in the wild, against 36–48 for earlier methods; [HaMeR](https://arxiv.org/html/2312.05251v1)). Use its wrist and four knuckle points for the knuckle centre. A crop cannot invent detail that a 480p frame never captured, so this complements better recording rather than replacing it. Super-resolution did reduce undetected hands in low-resolution sign-language footage ([Tasyurek et al., FG 2024](https://avesis.hacettepe.edu.tr/yayin/e0baeb7c-3cf0-44ef-aa53-5f181ad73caf/towards-better-communication-refining-hand-pose-estimation-in-low-resolution-sign-language-videos)). Fusing RTMW with a second model family is more promising than adding RTMW variants or more TTA modes. Averaging n estimates with correlation ρ leaves σ·√(ρ + (1−ρ)/n), so three models cut noise by about 10% at ρ = 0.7 (same family) and about 27% at ρ = 0.3 (different families). Weight each source by its measured stillness noise. Avoid learned temporal smoothers such as SmoothNet for confirmatory work: they cut acceleration error sharply but barely change position error, and their authors acknowledge a smoothing bias ([SmoothNet](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2112.13715)). Smoothing toward an already-smooth suit would flatter agreement. A safer temporal step is to check the 6 Hz Butterworth cutoff against the FBX wrist spectrum, applied identically to both systems and inside the Monte Carlo.

The suit's wrist needs scrutiny too. A Smartsuit Pro II without gloves has **one IMU on the back of each hand** ([Rokoko SDK](https://sdk.rokoko.com/reference/products/smartsuit-pro-ii/)), so its finger bones are rigid in rest geometry and its "knuckle centre" is a fixed point on the hand bone. The only Rokoko wrist study found the default flexion axis **35–39° from the functional axis**, with flexion and deviation coupled at |r| ≈ 0.99 until functional calibration ([IEEE BSN 2026, Amrita](https://www.amrita.edu/?p=697902)). First, check the FBX: near-zero variance in the finger-bone local rotations means no gloves were worn. In that case, report the angle as "hand-segment orientation", use the calibration block's wrist sweep to fit offset and gain, and keep the wrist last in the testing order.

## Synchronise to a few milliseconds, then let the Monte Carlo carry the rest

Sync is not why the current takes fail. A lag error only produces disagreement while the hands move: the induced angle error scales with v·Δt/L. For a 0.25 m forearm at 1 m/s, that is **1.9° at 8 ms, 3.8° at 17 ms and 7.6° at 33 ms**, and double at 2 m/s ([calibration notes, computed](../research_notes/Improving%20single%20take%20equivalence/camera_calibration_setup.md)). The shoulder deficits appear in still, arm-raised holds, where lag has no effect. Sync does matter for elbows and wrists at peak speed, which is where an RMS is earned or lost. It also matters for the honesty of the bound, because lag uncertainty currently enters the Monte Carlo nowhere.

The current chain is sound in outline but leaves precision unused. Frame times already come from OpenCV's `CAP_PROP_POS_MSEC` rather than index/fps. The view lag is a wrist-speed cross-correlation refined by a **quarter-frame grid search** (±0.12 s, linear interpolation) that minimises moving-frame reprojection error. The FBX lag is a 3D wrist-speed cross-correlation with a parabolic peak. Both lags are then **held fixed in every Monte Carlo draw** (`conditional_equivalence.py`). Triangulation also runs on the first view's time grid, which in After is the 30 fps front phone. The literature bounds what is achievable at each level of effort:

| Method | Reported accuracy | Role for SignSure |
|---|---|---|
| RecSync / libsoftwaresync on matched Android phones | ≤ 250–300 µs, drift < 1.2 ms/min ([RecSync](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2107.00987); [Ansari et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1812.09366)) | Best video↔video option; requires two identical Camera2 phones at equal fps |
| LED clock visible to both cameras (RocSync, ~US$20) | 1.34 ms RMSE versus hardware sync ([RocSync](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.14948v1)) | Independent per-take ground truth for the algorithmic lag |
| Pose-based sync with continuous-time (spline) motion | < 0.26 frame average error ([ResearchGate record](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/364478069_Self-Supervised_Human_Pose_based_Multi-Camera_Video_Synchronization)) | What the existing refinement should reach once made continuous |
| Single flash or lamp | Frame-level, not sub-frame ([RocSync](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.14948v1)) | Coarse check only |
| Impulsive body event in IMU streams | ~4.8 ms impulsive vs ~17 ms clapping, IMU↔IMU ([Kügler 2026](https://repositum.tuwien.at/handle/20.500.12708/226907)) | Choose a slap over a clap |
| Audio cross-correlation; consumer timecode | Worse than one frame (11–15 px and ~55 px reprojection error vs ~1 px for the LED clock) ([RocSync](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.14948v1)) | Coarse initialiser only |

The improvements fall into three groups. **In software**, first verify the timestamps. Read per-frame presentation times and edit lists with `ffprobe`, because phones record variable frame rate ([ProVideo Coalition](https://www.provideocoalition.com/understanding-iphone-framerates-for-shooting-editing-distribution/)) and an ignored MP4 edit list alone has produced 0.18–0.27 s offsets ([chronon PR #37](https://github.com/MerlanBromisch/chronon/pull/37)). Next, replace the quarter-frame grid with a continuous one-dimensional optimisation of the reprojection cost on cubic-spline-interpolated keypoints, and report the cost curvature as a local precision. RocSync showed that interpolating keypoints to sub-frame times cut 30 fps reprojection error from 3.07 to 1.02 px ([RocSync](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.14948v1)), and modelling sub-frame offsets significantly improved body fitting in spatiotemporal bundle adjustment ([arXiv 2007.12806](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2007.12806)). Use the 60 fps view as the time base. Estimate the FBX lag from several rotation-invariant signals — wrist speed, the norm of forearm and upper-arm angular velocity (the analogue of gyro-norm alignment; [Sensors 2025](https://www.mdpi.com/1424-8220/25/15/4680)) and elbow-angle rate — and require them to agree within a few milliseconds. Never estimate it from agreement of the tested angles, which would fit the very quantity under test. Do not use dynamic time warping for the final alignment: it would warp away genuine disagreement. The model should be an offset plus, optionally, a linear clock-rate term. Rolling shutter adds a row-dependent delay over a 5–25 ms readout ([Linköping tutorial](https://www.cvl.isy.liu.se/education/tutorials/rolling-shutter-tutorial/pdf/Intro30m.pdf)). Keeping both phones in the same orientation makes most of it common to both views, so model it only after the other steps are done.

**In the protocol**, add a sharp downward hand slap onto the thigh before the pre-sign hold and after the post-sign hold. A slap moves the forearm and hand sensors impulsively and shows a velocity break in both videos. A clap mainly excites local upper-body motion and is weaker for IMU alignment ([Kügler 2026](https://repositum.tuwien.at/handle/20.500.12708/226907)), and sound reaches two phones at different times. Two events give two independent lags, and their difference measures drift and frame-rate error, as in RocSync's start/end fit. One pipeline detail must be fixed before this works. `sign_window` runs from the first to the last sustained movement above 5% of peak wrist speed. A slap, or the calibration block's arm raise, would therefore be swallowed into the sign window and would also raise the peak that sets the threshold. The pre-registration must define the segmentation: for example, record the calibration block as a separate session recording with phones and suit untouched (the `noise_recording` path already shares calibration across recordings), and restrict the sign window to the movement between the two still holds. **In hardware**, two identical Android phones running RecSync or OpenCamera Sensors at 60 fps give sub-millisecond video↔video sync. A DFKI sign-language group maintains a fork ([DFKI RecSync-android](https://github.com/DFKI-SignLanguage/RecSync-android)). A RocSync-style LED clock gives an independent check. Rokoko's Command API accepts SMPTE timecode on start and stop recording ([Rokoko examples](https://github.com/Rokoko/studio-command-api-examples)), but no evidence shows sub-frame alignment, so treat it as a coarse start label.

Finally, **propagate what remains**. Treat each lag as a random variable. Take its spread from the disagreement among the independent estimates (signals, start versus end events) or from a block bootstrap over time segments, and draw it in every Monte Carlo iteration alongside the calibration refits. Report a deterministic RMS(lag) curve over ±1 frame as well. This makes the bound slightly wider when sync is uncertain and leaves it unchanged when sync is tight, which is the honest direction.

## Spend alpha where it is earned, and replicate before generalising

The fixed sequence did its job in Action. A genuinely doubtful early angle blocked later claims. But the cost is visible. Shoulder abduction (upper bounds 2.7° and 3.5°) and the elbows (6.9° and 11.1°) would each have passed alone, yet they received zero alpha ([sequential testing report](Single%20take%20equivalence%20sequential%20testing.md)). Closed-testing theory offers procedures that keep strong familywise control without that all-or-nothing stop. The **graphical approach** of Bretz and colleagues contains Bonferroni, Holm, fixed-sequence, fallback and gatekeeping procedures as special cases, and its decisions do not depend on the order in which hypotheses are rejected ([Bretz et al., arXiv 1908.10262](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1908.10262)). The **fallback procedure** keeps a pre-set order but reserves some alpha for each later hypothesis, so a failure no longer ends testing ([NCT03336242 SAP](https://cdn.clinicaltrials.gov/large-docs/42/NCT03336242/SAP_001.pdf)). For SignSure, a tiered graph that starts most of α at trunk twist, passes it on to the shoulder, elbow and wrist groups on success, and keeps fallback weights on later groups would preserve the scientific priority while letting a clearly passing elbow be confirmed after a shoulder failure. Holm over the six groups is the simpler, order-free alternative. Its first test must clear α/6, which an angle with a 2.8° bound clears easily. The pipeline's Monte Carlo already yields all nine RMS values jointly in each draw, so a parametric graphical test could later use their correlation to recover the conservatism of Bonferroni-type weights ([Bretz et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1908.10262)). Avoid Hochberg and Hommel: their validity rests on positive dependence, which these derived statistics do not guarantee ([Ristl et al. 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5069608)). Whichever graph is chosen, select its weights by simulated power before the take, confirm with `single_take_simulation.py` that P(EQUIVALENT) at the margin stays at or below 5% per angle, and commit it. Re-running After and Action under it is exploratory.

Other statistical options buy less than they seem to. A Bayesian ROPE analysis with vague priors returns essentially the same posterior as the bootstrap. Its decision rule has been criticised as incoherent ([Etz et al.](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10562001)), and it gives no free power for one take. Limits of agreement or TDI₉₅ make good secondary reports, but as primary claims they are stricter, not looser: TDI₉₅ ≈ 1.96·RMS, so "95% of differences within 15°" needs an RMS of about **7.6°** or less, against the roughly 9° the RMS claim tolerates. Report the coverage probability CP(15°) as a descriptive, as recommended for agreement studies ([Parker et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7291585/)). Per-joint margins are where honesty bites hardest. The literature would justify a tighter trunk margin (trunk RMSE 1.8–5.9° in good IMU studies) and spans 2.2–30° for the wrist ([Poitras et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6479822/)). But proposing a looser wrist margin now, after watching wrists fail, would be margin-widening in all but name. Keep 15° for every angle. Report each upper bound as a margin-free number, so a reader can see that, for example, a trunk bound of 2.8° would pass any margin at or above 2.8°.

Anything beyond "this take" requires more takes, with the take as the unit. Frames within a take are autocorrelated to an effective n of about 2–7, so pooling frames repeats the error that frame-level bootstraps made. Bland and Altman showed that treating replicate pairs as independent gives limits that are too narrow ([Bland & Altman 2007](https://www-users.york.ac.uk/%7Emb55/meas/bland2007.pdf)). Taking each take's bias-corrected per-angle RMS as one observation, a one-sided test of mean RMS below 15° at 80% power needs roughly **3–4 takes if the mean is 9° with a between-take SD of 3°**, 6–8 takes at an SD of 5°, and 13–17 takes if the mean is 11–12° ([statistical design notes, computed](../research_notes/Improving%20single%20take%20equivalence/statistical_design.md)). A claim about ISL signs in general needs a random-effects model and a prediction interval below 15°. With few signs such intervals are imprecise and often uninformative: in 479 Cochrane meta-analyses the 95% prediction interval included null or opposite effects 72.4% of the time ([IntHout et al. 2016](https://bmjopen.bmj.com/content/6/7/e010247)). Expect to need about ten or more signs. With fewer, report sign-specific results and a fixed-effect pooled estimate labelled as conditional on those signs. The number of takes, the model and the pooling rule must be fixed in advance. Adding takes until one passes is optional stopping unless a group-sequential design is pre-registered.

## What to commit before the next take, and what stays exploratory

Every methodological change here was motivated by seeing After and Action, so none of them can be confirmatory on those takes. They become confirmatory only for takes recorded and analysed after a dated commit to `equivalence_margins.json` (and the code it names). The ranking below orders changes by how much of the roughly 9° true-disagreement budget they should recover, per unit of effort:

| Priority | Change | Fixes | Pre-registration status |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Measured intrinsics per phone; board extrinsics per take; self-calibration only as refinement and diagnostic | Baseline/focal instability (56.1° → 64.8°), elbow swings of ~6° | Must be committed before the next take; re-running After/Action is exploratory |
| 2 | Same shoulder-point and trunk-axis semantics in FBX and video | Arm-raised elevation deficit of 13–24° | Must be committed; definition change |
| 3 | Calibration block (rest, arms raised, elbow bend, wrist sweep) with offset + capped gain fitted only on it, re-estimated per Monte Carlo draw | Posture-dependent offsets; wrist axis error | Must be committed, including poses, functional form, gain cap and segmentation rule |

| 6 | Sync: ffprobe timestamps, continuous spline refinement on the 60 fps base, multi-signal FBX lag, slap events before and after, lag draws in the Monte Carlo | Motion-time disagreement at peak speed; unpropagated lag error | Sync rule and its uncertainty model must be committed; LED-clock or RecSync checks are diagnostic |
| 7 | Graphical or fallback multiplicity procedure with simulated weights | Zero alpha after an early failure | Must be committed and simulation-checked; exploratory on After/Action |
| 8 | Multi-take, multi-sign design with take-level RMS and pre-set n | Generalisation beyond one take | Must be pre-registered, including pooling and stopping rules |

Three standing safeguards from earlier work still apply. First, measure motion-time keypoint noise on 100–200 hand-labelled frames per take, stratified by wrist speed, because the bias correction under-corrects whenever hold-based noise understates motion-time noise ([15° report](Single%20take%20equivalence%20at%2015%20degrees.md)). Second, re-run the at-margin simulation for any changed method and confirm that false equivalence stays at or below 5%. Third, always publish raw RMS, the offsets, the maximum per-frame difference and the share of frames outside ±15° next to the decision. Some things are legitimate at any time because they decide nothing. These include descriptive per-angle bounds under alternative pipelines, the board-versus-self-calibration comparison, the finger-bone variance check for gloves, the pre/post-hold drift check and the RMS(lag) curve, provided they are labelled exploratory.

## Conclusion

Equivalence at ±15° is now limited less by the test than by what the two instruments mean by "the same angle" and by how firmly the cameras are pinned down. The After result made this concrete. A change to the hand point, made for good reasons, moved the camera solution far enough to push an unrelated elbow from a comfortable to a marginal pass. A protocol whose calibration depends on the body being measured cannot give stable answers, however careful its statistics. Board calibration and shared point definitions remove that coupling. Their value lies less in the degrees they recover than in making each take's result reproducible from its setup alone.

The second shift is from treating disagreement as a constant to treating it as a function of posture. Rest-hold offsets were the right first model, but the shoulder data show that the definition gap grows with elevation. A calibration block measured outside the sign is the only non-circular way to model that. If a take that follows the full protocol still fails, the failure becomes diagnostic: the calibration block, the pre/post drift check and the independent sync checks will indicate whether the suit, the video or the definition is responsible. A pass remains what it has always been, agreement with this video reference for this take, and that is a claim the next takes can earn without moving the margin.
