# Single-Take Equivalence Works, but Rarely Passes

The method works as a statistical procedure, and it is weaker as a measurement. Its core logic is legitimate: for one fixed performance, compare the suit curve with the video curve, take video uncertainty from an external error model, push that uncertainty through the whole pipeline by Monte Carlo, and accept equivalence only when the interval lies inside ±5°. This is how metrology makes a conformity decision about a single measured item, and moving to it from frame-level bootstrapping was the right decision. The weak point is the error model, not the test. Your in-house false-equivalence rate of 0% holds only under the noise model you assumed. Every piece of outside evidence says that model is optimistic in three ways. Keypoint error grows during fast, blurred signing. TTA spread misses errors that all augmented copies share. And two-view self-calibration cannot pin down focal length. Your real footage already shows a TTA lower bound of **1.7–3.7 px (1080p-equivalent)**, which is exactly where simulated power falls from 96% to 0%. The likely outcome on real takes is therefore "not shown equivalent", and in most cases that will be the correct answer. Suit-versus-optical elbow error alone often uses up most of a ±5° margin. The defensible claim is narrow: *for this recorded take, under a stated and audited video-error model, the RMS suit–video elbow-flexion difference is below 5°*. It says nothing about the suit's accuracy, about other signs, or about other signers. The changes that help most cost no extra performances. Measure motion-time keypoint error on frames from this take. Fix each phone's intrinsics once, offline. Treat sync, heavy tails and skeleton offsets as explicit entries in the uncertainty budget.

## The statistics are sound; the error model carries all the risk

The earlier approaches failed for reasons the literature predicts. A ~90-frame elbow curve with lag-1 autocorrelation near 0.9 has an effective sample size of roughly 2–7. Block-bootstrap and HAC methods rest on asymptotic theory and are known to be badly sized at that scale. Finite-sample HAC tests show size distortion or power deficiency, and fixed-b corrections have non-standard limiting distributions ([Preinerstorfer & Pötscher, arXiv 1409.1419](https://www.arxiv.org/pdf/1409.1419)). Block-bootstrap results are also highly sensitive to block length ([blocklength README](https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/blocklength/readme/README.html)). Your block-bootstrap false-equivalence rates of 11–34% and your powerless effective-n TOST are what that theory predicts. More fundamentally, a single sign is a deterministic, non-stationary movement, so "the long-run mean of the difference process" is not a target that generalises anywhere.

The conditional reformulation fixes this by changing the estimand. The parameter becomes the RMS (or mean, or maximum) discrepancy between suit and video *for this realised movement*. The randomness comes from the measurement process, not from frames treated as samples. GUM Supplement 1 (JCGM 101) is the standard Monte Carlo alternative to linearised uncertainty propagation ([JCGM 101 record](https://ccn-scc.ca/standardsdb/standards/8171424)). JCGM 106 turns the resulting distribution into an accept/reject decision on a single item, with the *specific consumer's risk* as the controlled quantity ([arXiv 2409.11912](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2409.11912)). Running TOST at α against a Monte Carlo distribution is a guard-banded acceptance rule: you accept only when the 1−2α interval sits inside ±δ. Guard bands matter. Under simple acceptance with normal errors, the false-accept probability can reach **about 50%** ([IUPAC summary of JCGM 106](https://publications.iupac.org/ci/2013/3502/pac.html)). Your simulation's 0% false-equivalence at the margin is the right kind of evidence: an operating-characteristic check of the decision rule. Including the calibration refits inside the Monte Carlo is a real strength. Your own simulation found that the linearised covariance underestimated yaw error about fivefold, and only a full-chain resample captures a flat, non-quadratic cost landscape.

The RMS primary claim has a useful structural property. Measurement noise inflates the observed RMS difference: its expected square is roughly the true squared discrepancy plus the noise variance. Noise therefore pushes toward non-equivalence. That is probably why the RMS claim keeps false equivalence near zero even when the noise model is wrong, while the mean claim does not. Your mean-CI coverage of **0.73–0.79** when motion noise exceeds rest noise means a nominal 90% interval is really closer to 75%. Under realistic noise, the mean claim is anti-conservative. The same property that protects the RMS claim also takes its power. Prespecifying RMS as primary was correct, and the mean claim should be reported only with a guard band that is sized for under-modelled noise.

The spm1d check over augmentation replicates is valid for a narrower hypothesis than it seems to address. The replicates sample only the pipeline's response to flips, scales and shifts. Random field theory assumes they are independent, identically distributed smooth Gaussian fields. Its validity also depends on smoothness assumptions that biomechanical data do not always meet ([Pataky et al. 2019](https://researchonline.ljmu.ac.uk/id/eprint/10760/1/Pataky%202019%20Heterogenity.pdf)). It is a consistency check on the primary claim, not independent confirmation. "No significant SPM cluster" must never be read as equivalence: in one 58-participant study, SPM found no differences while TOST still failed to confirm equivalence ([Appiah et al. 2026](https://consensus.app/papers/details/fd01007b589b5e089acb1b3d735cca53/?utm_source=claude_code)). The whole-curve simultaneous band is structurally the band-inclusion equivalence test of the functional-data literature. The max-deviation test of Dette & Kokot is reported to be less conservative and more powerful than band-based tests ([arXiv 2004.12364](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.12364v1)), and it is worth adopting if the band is kept.

## Real footage sits where the simulation's power collapses

Power depends on keypoint noise to an extreme degree. It is 96% at 1.5 px, 29% when motion noise is 1.5× rest noise, and 0% at 3 px. Your measured TTA spread of 3.3–7.5 px at 4K, about 1.7–3.7 px at 1080p and largest at the wrists and index fingers, is a *lower bound* on true error. It already overlaps the region where power collapses. Elbow flexion depends directly on the wrist keypoint, so the noisiest joint dominates.

The literature says the true error is larger than the TTA spread, for three separate reasons. First, motion makes keypoints worse. On corrupted COCO benchmarks, motion blur cuts HRNet-W48 from 75.6 to 43.7 mAP and ViTPose-H from 78.8 to 46.4 ([PoseBench, arXiv 2406.14367](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.14367v1)). MediaPipe misses more than half of WLASL hands, which the authors attribute to blur from rapid movement ([SignLang 2024](https://aclanthology.org/2024.signlang-1.36.pdf)). No study gives a pixel-level speed multiplier for RTMW, but the direction is consistent across every source found. Your 1.5× motion/rest scenario is a plausible central case, not a pessimistic edge case. Second, TTA cannot see errors that every augmented copy shares. Blur, occlusion and label-convention bias are identical in all copies, so they add nothing to the spread. Pose confidence is itself poorly calibrated: keypoint-level correlation between confidence and OKS is only **r ≈ 0.64–0.78** ([Gu et al., CVPR 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2311.17105)). Third, top-down whole-body models never output "missing". In an eight-model signing benchmark, the MMPose whole-body family reported 0% missing hands but posed only **33%** of occlusion cases correctly ([arXiv 2604.24609](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.24609)). Its failures appear as confident wrong points, which are heavy-tailed outliers, not inflated Gaussian jitter.

Temporal structure makes this worse. SmoothNet documents "long-term jitter": persistent multi-frame errors that become entangled with bias and that low-pass filters cannot remove ([SmoothNet, arXiv 2112.13715](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2112.13715)). An AR(1) coefficient estimated on still frames will understate persistence during hard segments. The 6 Hz filter then removes the independent part of the noise and keeps the correlated part, which is exactly the part that does not average out over the sign. The practical consequence is asymmetric. Under-modelled noise mostly costs power on the RMS claim, but it invalidates the stated coverage of the mean and band claims.

One implication is easy to miss. RTMW runs on a person crop resized to 384×288 ([RTMW, arXiv 2407.08634](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.08634)). Noise relative to limb length is therefore set mostly by the model's input resolution and the framing, not by whether the phone records at 4K or 1080p. Recording at higher resolution will not, by itself, buy back power. Reducing blur, framing the arms more tightly, or running a dedicated high-resolution arm or hand crop will.

## Two uncalibrated phones cannot identify focal length

The self-calibration combines published ideas sensibly: keypoint bundle adjustment as in CasCalib, bone-length constancy as in ElliPose and Tresadern & Reid, and a focal prior. But it operates in the least-validated regime. CasCalib's authors state that **two views give too few constraints for bundle adjustment under noisy keypoints**, and their experiments used four cameras ([CasCalib, arXiv 2405.06845](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.06845v1)). The best human-as-calibration results, 0.41° rotation error, assume known intrinsics and use about 30 cameras ([arXiv 2412.19089](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.19089v1)). Pose2Sim's keypoint calibration is still marked "coming soon", estimates extrinsics only, and takes intrinsics from a board ([Pose2Sim](https://github.com/perfanalytics/pose2sim)). No peer-reviewed study was found that validates joint angles from a two-camera, keypoint-only self-calibrated setup against optical mocap.

The focal-length problem is structural. Two-view focal estimation is singular when the optical axes are coplanar, and two tripods aimed at one torso at similar heights are close to that configuration. On real data, two-view methods reach median focal errors of **15–25%**, and a fixed prior alone (f = 1.2 × image size) gets about 20% ([Kocur et al., arXiv 2311.16304](https://arxiv.org/html/2311.16304v3)). Your ±15% prior is therefore roughly as informative as the data, and in practice the prior sets the focal length. This matters for the Monte Carlo. Refitting calibrations from resampled keypoints captures the *variance* of the calibration, but if each refit pulls toward the same prior centre, it cannot capture the *bias* that prior introduces. Focal error scales depth, so elbow flexion in the front-view image plane is fairly robust, while forearm-toward-camera flexion, which is common in signing, is the most sensitive.

The 30–45° oblique baseline is also narrower than the validated configuration. OpenCap found two iPhones at about ±45° (roughly 90° apart) as good as three to five cameras, but that result relied on checkerboard extrinsics and per-device intrinsics ([Uhlrich et al. 2023](https://journals.plos.org/ploscompbiol/article?id=10.1371/journal.pcbi.1011462)). Synchronisation is a further unlisted error source. In CasCalib, a 50-frame offset left unsolved raised rotation error from 2.2° to **13.1°** ([arXiv 2405.06845](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.06845v1)). Residual sub-frame offsets, phone rolling shutter, and digital stabilisation warps, which change image geometry frame by frame ([Karpenko et al. 2011](https://graphics.stanford.edu/papers/stabilization/)), all add wrist triangulation error that keypoint jitter does not model. At a wrist speed of 1 m/s, a 16 ms offset produces about 16 mm of epipolar inconsistency. That is comparable to a full system's marker error.

## A ±5° margin is tight before video error even enters

The margin follows convention. RMSE up to 5° is "regarded as reasonable" by most kinematic validation studies ([Choo et al. 2022](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0262730)). The problem is that both systems are being compared, and neither is a gold standard. Even with ten calibrated cameras, Theia3D's elbow flexion shows **7.0–7.4° RMSD with −5 to −6° bias** against markers ([Lahkar et al. 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/sports-and-active-living/articles/10.3389/fspor.2022.939980/full)). IMU suits do worse on absolute elbow angle. Perception Neuron's elbow RMSE was below 4°, but its Bland–Altman bias exceeded 10° in every movement ([Choo et al. 2022](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0262730)). A meta-analysis pooled an IMU-versus-optical elbow mean difference of **10.6°** ([Li et al. 2024](https://consensus.app/papers/details/e18407ddfaca55d5baddb82df791b838/?utm_source=claude_code)). Rokoko has essentially no peer-reviewed upper-limb validation.

The arithmetic is unforgiving. If suit and video errors are independent, the expected RMS difference is about √(suit² + video²). A 4° suit and a 4° video system give about 5.7°, which fails a 5° margin even though both systems are individually "reasonable". Passing requires both to be better than about 3.5°. Skeleton definition adds on top of this. Between-system angle differences are dominated by model and coordinate-system definitions, not sensor error: Xsens upper-limb RMSE reached 15° against under 5° when segment definitions matched ([Mavor et al. 2020](https://consensus.app/papers/details/a2c52d469d7d5d04b1efdf90a3160fca/?utm_source=claude_code); [Robert-Lachaine et al. 2017](https://unpaywall.org/10.1007%2FS11517-016-1537-2)). Pose-model joint centres also carry systematic offsets of 30–50 mm at some joints ([Needham et al. 2021](https://consensus.app/papers/details/aa33d7bd92135d3fbbb6de2b7237908d/?utm_source=claude_code)). A Mixamo-style retargeted elbow is a bone rotation relative to a rest pose, not an anatomical angle. A constant offset of several degrees is plausible before any noise is added. This is an inference, since no study was found that measured FBX retarget error directly.

Two consequences follow. A pass is strong evidence for this take, because the RMS test is conservative and the true discrepancy has to be small. A fail tells you nothing about *which* system is wrong. Studies that compare two indirect systems describe the result as "an upper bound on the error", not as accuracy ([Francia et al. 2026, Sensors](https://consensus.app/papers/details/0cd0a4a588bd51eaa1a1ee7e867a5563/?utm_source=claude_code)). Whatever the outcome, the result is a method-agreement statement. It is not a validation of the suit.

## What the method can and cannot claim

| Claim | Defensible? | Condition |
|---|---|---|
| RMS suit–video elbow difference < 5° for *this take* | Yes (primary) | Error model disclosed; tipping-point sensitivity reported |
| Mean difference within ±5° for this take | Weak | Coverage drops to ~0.75 under realistic motion noise; needs inflated noise or guard band |
| Whole curve inside ±5° band | Yes, but low power | Same model caveats; max-deviation test preferable |
| spm1d over TTA replicates confirms equivalence | No, consistency check only | Replicates sample augmentation noise, not measurement error |
| Suit is accurate to within 5° | No | Video is not a criterion; needs optical or goniometer anchor |
| Video agrees with suit for other signs, signers, setups | No | Agreement varies by joint, plane and task ([Scataglini et al. 2024](https://consensus.app/papers/details/b2ea274d06605ac19019c898cf994898/?utm_source=claude_code)) |
| Population limits of agreement, TDI, CCC, COSMIN-grade measurement error | No | Requires independent replicates; COSMIN rates fewer than 30 participants as small ([PMC7343224](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7343224/table/t3-ejr-7-2-71)) |

The precise wording should read like a conformity statement. For example: "For recorded take X, under the stated video measurement-error model (keypoint σ, persistence, calibration, sync), the RMS elbow-flexion difference between suit and video is below 5°, with false-acceptance probability at most 5% under that model; the claim survives keypoint noise up to k× the measured rest level." The final clause is the most useful addition, because it moves the unverifiable assumption out into the open. No published biomechanics or pose study was found that validates a system with n = 1 through a conditional equivalence argument. Reviewers will treat the framing as novel and will press hardest on how good the error model is.

## Ranked changes that would make it work better

The ranking below weighs how much each change protects validity or recovers power against whether it breaks the single-take constraint. The first five need no additional performance.

| Rank | Change | What it fixes | Single-take compatible? |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Hand-label wrist, elbow and shoulder on 200–400 frames *of this take*, oversampling fast, blurred and hand-over-face frames; fit σ_motion directly or conformally scale TTA (find k so k·σ_TTA covers 90% of errors) | Replaces the untested "motion ≤ rest" assumption with a measurement | Yes |
| 2 | Calibrate each phone's intrinsics once with a checkerboard or ChArUco, then lock focus and zoom, disable stabilisation, and keep the lens fixed | Removes the non-identifiable focal length, the dominant calibration bias | Yes (done separately from the performance) |
| 3 | Add a tipping-point sensitivity grid (motion/rest 1.0, 1.5, 2.0, 3.0; outlier rate 0–5%) to the prespecified report | Makes the claim honest about its key assumption | Yes |
| 4 | Upgrade the noise model: speed-conditional σ, Gaussian-plus-outlier mixture, persistence estimated in motion; use two-view reprojection residuals as a per-frame floor (σ = max of still, TTA, residual) | Captures heavy tails and occlusion errors that TTA cannot see | Yes |
| 5 | Add sync offset (and rolling-shutter) draws to the Monte Carlo; compute the suit angle from FBX joint *positions* with the same vector definition; prespecify raw bias and offset-removed RMS as separate reported quantities | Turns hidden systematic sources into budgeted ones | Yes |
| 6 | Widen the oblique view toward ~90° separation, add camera elevation difference, frame arms tightly, use a high shutter speed and 60 fps or more | Better depth conditioning and less blur, recovering power | Yes (setup only) |
| 7 | Use a dedicated higher-resolution arm/hand crop or cross-model ensemble (RTMW plus a second whole-body model) as a further noise check | Lowers effective σ; inter-model disagreement adds a lower-bound signal ([arXiv 2604.24609](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.24609)) | Yes |

Change 2 conflicts with the "no checkerboard" premise only on the surface. Intrinsics belong to the phone and its locked settings, not to the scene, so they can be measured any day without touching the performance. OpenCap uses a per-device intrinsic database for exactly this reason, and found an A4-printed board sufficient for extrinsics ([Uhlrich et al. 2023](https://journals.plos.org/ploscompbiol/article?id=10.1371/journal.pcbi.1011462)). If one A4 board can appear in the scene for a single frame, the extrinsics problem goes away too. Changes 1 and 3 together address the method's single biggest exposure. Your simulation shows the decision going from 96% to 0% power as noise doubles, so the claim is only as credible as the noise level you can demonstrate.

Extra recordings buy different kinds of claim, not just more precision. A few static holds at goniometer-measured elbow angles, recorded with both systems, give partial ground truth for *each* system separately. That is the cheapest way to make a failed equivalence result informative about which side is wrong. A second take of the same sign by the same signer gives a between-take variance and lets you test whether the agreement repeats. Even a brief anchor session against optical capture, the accepted criterion, would let you report suit and video accuracy rather than only their discrepancy. A generalisable claim about the system needs the field's usual design of 10–30 participants with repeated trials ([Pedro et al. 2021](https://consensus.app/papers/details/133fee8efeb95b75a503b7f83cb160cb/?utm_source=claude_code); [Choo et al. 2022](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0262730)), analysed with curve-level functional TOST across takes ([Fogarty & Small 2014](https://arxiv.org/abs/1407.5079)) or repeated-measures agreement models. In that design, today's single-take analysis becomes the per-take building block.

## Conclusion

The method is best understood as a measurement-uncertainty argument, not a sampling argument. That reframing decides where effort should go. A more sophisticated test will not make the claim stronger. Evidence that the input error model is not optimistic will: labelled motion frames, fixed intrinsics, and a reported tipping point. The conservatism of the RMS claim means false equivalence is not the main danger. The main danger is a procedure that almost never says yes on real signing footage, and so cannot separate a good video pipeline from a bad one.

The comparison itself also deserves rethinking. With no gold standard on either side, and with literature biases of 5–10° at the elbow for both suits and markerless systems, a ±5° RMS margin between two indirect systems demands more than either has shown on its own. Splitting the claim into a bias component, anchored by a few static goniometer poses, and an offset-removed shape component would likely turn an uninformative fail into two interpretable results. It would also keep the single-performance design intact for the dynamic part.
