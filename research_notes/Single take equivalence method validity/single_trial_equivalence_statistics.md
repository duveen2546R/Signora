Scope note: the Consensus search tool was rate-limited for the first batch and on the free tier returns only 3 results per query, so most sources are from web search. Items labelled "[background knowledge, not verified this session]" are standard references I could not fetch or confirm here. Treat them as leads to check, not as cited facts.

## Q1. TOST / equivalence testing with autocorrelated data and small effective sample size (effective n, HAC/Newey-West, block bootstrap, prewhitening)

### Takeaway
The standard toolkit (effective-n corrections, HAC/Newey-West, block bootstrap) is built on asymptotics. It is known to be fragile or badly sized when the series is short and strongly persistent, and the fragility is worst at an effective n of about 2 to 7. The user's finding (block-bootstrap false-equivalence rates of 11 to 34%; an effective-n TOST that is valid but has no power) is what this literature predicts. None of these methods turns ~90 frames of one performance into evidence about other performances.

### Cited Findings
- TOST rejects non-equivalence at level α only if both one-sided nulls are rejected at α. The overall p-value is the larger of the two one-sided p-values, and no multiplicity correction is needed because at most one of the two nulls can be true. — [JMP Equivalence Test docs](https://www.jmp.com/support/help/Equivalence_Test.shtml); [TOSTER reference manual](https://archive.linux.duke.edu/cran/web/packages/TOSTER/refman/TOSTER.html)
- Bretherton et al. (1999), J. Climate 12:1990–2009, Section 5, covers the effective sample size of autocorrelated time series and its use in significance testing. — [Bretherton et al. 1999 PDF](https://www2.kaiyodai.ac.jp/~mizobata/ds/clim-1520-0442_1999_012_1990_tenosd_2.0.co_2.pdf)
- In practice, the effective-n approach replaces n in the t-statistic with the effective sample size, computed from lag-1 autocorrelations. — [climpred metrics docs](https://climpred.readthedocs.io/en/v2.1.3/metrics.html); [xskillscore effective_sample_size](https://xskillscore.readthedocs.io/en/latest/api/xskillscore.effective_sample_size.html)
- Vecchi et al. report that the general Bretherton two-series formula was mis-transcribed in their own 2013 paper, and the error underestimated the degrees of freedom. The formula is easy to get wrong in implementation. — [Vecchi bibliography entry](https://vecchi.princeton.edu/bibcite/reference/3041)
- Zwiers & von Storch (1995), "Taking serial correlation into account in tests of the mean", J. Climate 8:336–351, is the standard reference on t-tests of the mean under serial correlation. The NCL implementation returns a reduced equivalent sample size only when the lag-1 autocorrelation is significant, and assumes equal time spacing. — [NCL equiv_sample_size](https://www.ncl.ucar.edu/Document/Functions/Built-in/equiv_sample_size.shtml)
- HAC tests suffer size distortions and power deficiencies in finite samples, which motivated fixed-b asymptotics (bandwidth set as a fixed fraction b of the sample size). Under fixed-b, HAC test statistics have pivotal but non-standard limiting distributions that depend on the kernel and b, so normal critical values are wrong. — [arXiv 1409.1419 (Preinerstorfer & Pötscher)](https://www.arxiv.org/pdf/1409.1419); [Hansen, Princeton seminar paper](https://www.princeton.edu/~erp/erp%20seminar%20pdfs/papersspring09/hansen.pdf)
- Preinerstorfer & Pötscher argue that, for generic designs, prewhitened and some fixed-b-style tests show either extreme size distortion or strong power deficiency. They note that Kiefer & Vogelsang (2005) and Rho & Shao (2013) suggest prewhitening can improve the finite-sample behaviour of fixed-b tests. — [arXiv 1409.1419](https://www.arxiv.org/pdf/1409.1419)
- The Politis–White (2004) automatic block length rests on flat-top spectral estimation and asymptotic optimality. It was corrected by Patton, Politis & White (2009) after Nordman (2008) corrected Lahiri's theory. Block-bootstrap results are highly sensitive to block length. — [Politis & White 2004 record](https://gnosis.library.ucy.ac.cy/handle/7/57548); [Correction record](https://gnosis.library.ucy.ac.cy/handle/7/57476); [blocklength R package README](https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/blocklength/readme/README.html)

### Inferences
- With lag-1 r ≈ 0.9 and 90 frames, the optimal block length is a sizeable fraction of the series. Only a handful of quasi-independent blocks exist, so the bootstrap distribution is too narrow (under-coverage), and an undersized CI makes TOST anti-conservative. This explains the 11–34% false-equivalence rates. The block-bootstrap theory is asymptotic, and I found no source claiming validity at effective n of about 2–7.
- An effective-n t-test with n_eff ≈ 2–7 has t-critical values with 1–6 df. The resulting CIs are very wide, which explains the near-zero power. The effective-n result is the honest description of how much *within-trial* information the residual series carries about its own long-run mean.
- More fundamentally, all of these methods treat d(t) as one realisation of a stationary process and make inference about that process's mean. For a single sign, d(t) is driven by a deterministic, non-stationary movement, so "the process mean" is not a well-defined target that generalises beyond this movement. That is the main argument for the user's switch to conditional inference.
- Prewhitening (fitting AR(1) and testing the innovations) helps the variance estimate but does not add information. With n_eff this small, the AR coefficient itself is poorly estimated, which is the reason the user applied a Kendall bias correction.

### Gaps
- I found no simulation study reporting TOST size and power under AR(1) with n_eff < 10. The user's own simulation appears to be the main evidence on this point.
- Lakens (2017, Soc Psychol Personal Sci) on TOST, and Wellek's textbook on equivalence testing with dependent observations, were not fetched this session. [background knowledge, not verified this session]

## Q2. Functional / curve equivalence (SPM1d, functional TOST, simultaneous bands, interval-wise testing) and the number of curves needed

### Takeaway
Functional equivalence tests do exist: functional TOST with the bootstrap (Fogarty & Small 2014), simultaneous-band and maximum-deviation tests (Dette and colleagues; Dette & Kokot 2021), and ad hoc SPM-plus-TOST pipelines in biomechanics. All of them treat each *curve* as the sampling unit, with replicate curves from many subjects or trials. None treats the frames within one curve as the sample. I found no documented native equivalence/TOST routine in spm1d itself.

### Cited Findings
- Fogarty & Small (2014), Annals of Applied Statistics 8(4):2002–2026, extend TOST to functional data and carry it out with the nonparametric bootstrap. They also give a Bayesian functional-ANOVA alternative. The application is a method comparison of two pulmonary function devices. — [arXiv 1407.5079](https://arxiv.org/abs/1407.5079)
  - Note: one search-engine summary attributed this paper to Liebl & Reimherr. The arXiv page shows the authors are Fogarty & Small.
- Dette & Kokot (2021), Biometrika 108:895–913, test equivalence of functional parameters (mean or variance function) in the two-sample setting. Equivalence is claimed if the estimated maximum deviation falls below a threshold, with bootstrap quantiles. The approach avoids intersection–union and is less conservative and more powerful than band-based tests. — [arXiv 2004.12364](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.12364v1); [IDEAS record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/biomet/v108y2021i4p895-913..html)
- Earlier functional equivalence tests decide via simultaneous confidence bands that must lie inside the equivalence bands (Dette et al. 2020; Liebl & Reimherr 2019, cited in Dette & Kokot). — [arXiv 2004.12364](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2004.12364)
- Pataky introduced spm1d (2010/2012) for whole-curve inference with random field theory. Inference is cluster-based against a null of *no difference*. — [spm1d references](https://spm1d.org/References.html); [Pataky et al. 2019, SPM validity for non-uniformly smooth data](https://researchonline.ljmu.ac.uk/id/eprint/10760/1/Pataky%202019%20Heterogenity.pdf); [spm1d ISB 2017 slides](https://spm1d.org/_downloads/793453c1fd63a0f274ac3bd67fbd99cb/Slides-ISB2017.pdf)
- Pataky, Vanrenterghem, Robinson & Liebl (2019) examine whether SPM inference is valid for non-uniformly smooth 1D biomechanical data. RFT validity depends on smoothness assumptions. — [LJMU repository PDF](https://researchonline.ljmu.ac.uk/id/eprint/10760/1/Pataky%202019%20Heterogenity.pdf)
- A recent biomechanics example combines SPM with TOST on GRF waveforms in 58 participants. TOST did not confirm equivalence within ±0.2 N/kg even though SPM found no significant clusters. Absence of an SPM difference is not equivalence. — [Appiah et al. 2026, BMC Biomed Eng](https://consensus.app/papers/details/fd01007b589b5e089acb1b3d735cca53/?utm_source=claude_code)
- Power for 1D SPM tests depends on noise amplitude and smoothness (FWHM), signal amplitude and width, region-of-interest use, and paired design. Tabulated sample sizes are in units of participants. — [Luciano et al. 2021, J Biomech](https://consensus.app/papers/details/9bebeee75a35522b83bf708b4f8722f7/?utm_source=claude_code)
- Markerless-versus-marker validation studies typically use RMSD averaged over cycles plus SPM paired t-tests, with multiple participants and trials (for example 10 participants × 5 trials). — [Cameron-Whytock et al. 2025, J Biomech](https://consensus.app/papers/details/0504f1c37a9b5e729dda59290ffa997e/?utm_source=claude_code)
- Spm1d was searched for a TOST/equivalence function, but none was documented in the sources found. — [spm1d references](https://spm1d.org/References.html)

### Inferences
- The user's secondary check (spm1d one-sample TOST over test-time-augmentation replicates) uses augmentation replicates as "curves". These replicates sample only the video pipeline's response to perturbations, not between-performance or between-session variation. The spm1d variance therefore estimates augmentation noise, and the RFT threshold assumes the replicates are i.i.d. smooth Gaussian fields. That makes it a valid test of a narrower hypothesis: "under this augmentation noise model, the mean video curve for this take lies within ±δ of the suit curve." Its reach is the same as the primary conditional claim, not wider. It should be described as a consistency check, not as independent confirmation.
- The studentised-max simultaneous band in the user's method is structurally the same construction as the band-inclusion equivalence tests (Liebl & Reimherr; Dette et al. 2020). The difference is that the user's variability comes from a parametric measurement-error model, not from replicate subjects. Methodologically this is defensible; the population of inference is what differs.
- Interval-wise testing (Pini & Vantini) is another option for localising where the curves differ, but it was not verified this session. [background knowledge, not verified this session]

### Gaps
- No minimum number of curves for functional TOST was found in the sources fetched. The Fogarty & Small abstract does not give one.
- Whether Fogarty & Small use pointwise or simultaneous intervals was not confirmed from the abstract.

## Q3. Parametric bootstrap / Monte Carlo propagation (GUM-S1 style) as the basis of a hypothesis test; conditional inference on a single realisation

### Takeaway
This is the strongest pillar of the method. In metrology, a single measurement with uncertainty evaluated from an external, independently characterised error model is the normal case, not the exception. JCGM 101 (GUM Supplement 1) sanctions Monte Carlo propagation of distributions. JCGM 106 turns the resulting distribution into an accept/reject decision with controlled specific consumer's risk, using guard bands. The user's "conditional on this performance" claim maps closely onto a conformity-assessment decision about one item. Its validity rests entirely on how correct and complete the input error model is.

### Cited Findings
- JCGM 101:2008 (GUM Supplement 1, "Propagation of distributions using a Monte Carlo method") is the official alternative to the linearised GUM. — [ISO/IEC Guide 98-3 Suppl 1 record](https://ccn-scc.ca/standardsdb/standards/8171424); [NPL eprint](https://eprintspublications.npl.co.uk/4640/); [arXiv 1012.2631, Monte Carlo uncertainty for wavelength calibration](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1012.2631)
- The GUM-S1 procedure evaluates the measurement model for M Monte Carlo draws of the inputs. The output estimate is the mean and the uncertainty is the SD of the draws, with coverage intervals taken from the empirical distribution. — [MetricGate GUM-S1 overview (secondary)](https://metricgate.com/docs/gum-s1-monte-carlo-uncertainty/)
- JCGM 106:2012 defines conformity decisions on a single measured item. The posterior conformance probability is the indicator, and the *specific consumer's risk* is the probability that an accepted item is actually non-conforming. — [arXiv 2409.11912, Conformity assessment within JCGM 106](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2409.11912)
- Guard bands (acceptance limits set inside the tolerance limits, often sized by k = 2 expanded uncertainty) reduce false acceptance at the cost of more false rejection. Under simple acceptance with normal errors, the false-accept probability can reach about 50%. — [IUPAC Chemistry International summary of JCGM 106](https://publications.iupac.org/ci/2013/3502/pac.html); [ACTA IMEKO article](https://acta.imeko.org/index.php/acta-imeko/article/view/1468/2813)
- Specific risks can be computed component by component for multi-property items. — [Kuselman et al. 2019, J Phys Conf Ser](https://iris.inrim.it/retrieve/handle/11696/61207/dd2573c2-b61b-e71c-e053-d805fe0ad5dc/Kuselman_2019_J._Phys.__Conf._Ser._1420_012001.pdf)
- Monte Carlo-based conformity decisions are taught operationally (LNE course; CASoft tool), and EURAMET has listed conformity-assessment decision procedures as a research need. — [LNE ME13 course](https://www.lne.fr/sites/default/files/pdf/ME13-01-2024.pdf)

### Inferences
- In the user's method, "TOST at α using the Monte Carlo sampling distribution" is equivalent to the JCGM 106 practice of accepting when the 1−2α (or one-sided 1−α) coverage interval lies inside the tolerance ±δ. That is a guard-banded acceptance rule, and its false-accept rate is controlled *only under the assumed error model*. The user's simulation (0% false equivalence at the margin; power 96% at 1.5 px and 0% at 3 px) is the right type of evidence: an operating-characteristic check of the decision rule under that model.
- The central vulnerability is model adequacy. Keypoint jitter from still frames and TTA replicates capture *random, high-frequency* video error. They may miss error that is systematic for this performance: motion blur, self-occlusion during fast signing, depth ambiguity in elbow flexion from 2D views, temporal misalignment between suit and video, and suit (IMU/optical) error, which the video model does not cover. Under GUM, unmodelled systematic effects bias the result and are not covered by the stated uncertainty. A defensible write-up should include an explicit uncertainty budget that lists suit error, synchronisation error, and triangulation/calibration error, or should state that they were assumed negligible and why.
- The approach is a conditional (frequentist, given the fixed true trajectory) or "fixed-effect" inference. The parameter is the RMS/mean/max discrepancy *for this realised movement*. That is a legitimate estimand. Its inference population is repeated measurement of the same performance by the same systems, not new signers, signs, or sessions.
- Keeping the upper bound of the RMS noise-inflated is conservative in the right direction for an equivalence claim, because it biases toward non-equivalence. This mirrors guard banding.

### Gaps
- I did not fetch the JCGM 101 or 106 text directly. Quotes come from secondary summaries.
- I found no source applying GUM-S1 Monte Carlo explicitly as a TOST for method comparison in biomechanics or pose estimation.

## Q4. RMS / TDI / CCC / coverage probability as agreement criteria and their single-subject applicability

### Takeaway
Lin's TDI and the coverage probability (CP) are formally "unscaled" agreement indices defined on the distribution of paired differences, and they are tested as equivalence-type hypotheses (for example, TDI_p < δ). Their sampling theory is built for many independent paired readings, usually across subjects. Even then, Choudhary & Nagaraja showed that Lin's TDI test can be inconsistent and that CP can be overly conservative at moderate n. The user's frame-wise RMS over one sign is closest to an "MSD/TDI over time" for one subject, which is meaningful descriptively but has no standard single-subject sampling theory.

### Cited Findings
- Lin (2000), Stat Med 19:255–270, defines the TDI κ_p as the boundary containing a large proportion p of paired differences between two devices. — [Escaramís et al. 2010, PMC2859350](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC2859350/); [MethComp TDI documentation](https://rdrr.io/cran/MethComp/man/TDI.html)
- Lin, Hedayat, Sinha & Yang (2002), JASA 97:257–270, define the coverage probability: the probability that (X, Y) falls in a strip of width 2d around X = Y. — [Escaramís et al. 2010, PMC2859350](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2859350)
- Choudhary & Nagaraja (2005, JSPI) show that Lin's TDI approach is not consistent and may not preserve its nominal level, and that the CP approach is overly conservative at moderate n. They propose an exact test and a bootstrap-t test for n > 30. — [Choudhary & Nagaraja 2005 preprint](https://personal.utdallas.edu/~pankaj/choudhary_nagaraja_jspi_2005_preprint.pdf)
- Escaramís, Ascaso & Carrasco (2010) estimate the TDI with tolerance intervals and give CP bounds. Lin, Pan, Hedayat, Barnhart & Haber (2016) give a non-parametric TDI via quantile regression. — [PMC2859350](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC2859350/); [UB repository record](https://diposit.ub.edu/dspace/handle/2445/12762)

### Inferences
- These indices all assume i.i.d. paired readings. Applied to frames of one sign, the "proportion of differences within δ" is a property of this curve (a deterministic time fraction), not a population coverage. The user's simultaneous max band is a stricter cousin: it implies TDI_1.0 < δ for this take.
- RMS over the sign is a sensible primary metric. It matches the RMSD that the biomechanics validation literature reports (for example Cameron-Whytock et al. 2025) and is the square root of Lin's MSD. Framing it as "MSD-based agreement conditional on the trajectory" links it to the Lin/Barnhart taxonomy.
- The concordance correlation coefficient (CCC) is ill-suited here. It is scaled by between-"subject" variance, which for a single curve is the range of motion of that sign, so it would reward large movements.

### Gaps
- Barnhart, Haber & Lin (2007) on agreement indices and Choudhary & Nagaraja's book "Measuring Agreement" (Wiley 2017) were not fetched. [background knowledge, not verified this session]
- No source found addresses TDI or CP for a single subject's time series.

## Q5. n-of-1 / single-case validation in biomechanics; what COSMIN and GRRAS say about one participant or trial

### Takeaway
Measurement-property guidelines treat the number of independent participants as central to generalisable claims. COSMIN rates fewer than 30 participants as a "small" sample (risk-of-bias rating for reliability), and GRRAS requires the sample size to be justified and the numbers of raters, subjects, and replicates to be reported. A single take cannot support a claim about the measurement system's agreement in general (across signers, signs, sessions, camera setups). It can support a well-specified conditional claim about that take, which is closer to a calibration/verification or proof-of-concept result.

### Cited Findings
- COSMIN reliability and measurement-error studies require repeated measurements in stable individuals (at least two independent measurements under similar conditions). Measurement error is defined as within-person variation. — [Mokkink et al. 2020, BMC Med Res Methodol](https://bmcmedresmethodol.biomedcentral.com/track/pdf/10.1186/s12874-020-01179-5); [Mokkink 2025 measurement paper](https://publications.tno.nl/publication/34645296/odPbhDAW/mokkink-2025-measurement.pdf)
- COSMIN sample-size ratings for reliability: 100 or more is adequate, 50–99 good, 30–49 moderate, and under 30 small. These are risk-of-bias ratings, not power calculations. — [COSMIN Box table, PMC7343224](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7343224/table/t3-ejr-7-2-71)
- The COSMIN Risk of Bias tool for reliability and measurement error was developed by Delphi consensus. It covers 5 design standards plus preferred statistical methods. — [Mokkink et al. 2020](https://bmcmedresmethodol.biomedcentral.com/track/pdf/10.1186/s12874-020-01179-5)
- GRRAS (Kottner et al. 2011, J Clin Epidemiol 64:96–106) has 15 reporting items. Item 6 asks authors to explain how the sample size was chosen and to state the numbers of raters, subjects/objects, and replicate observations. Item 13 asks for estimates with uncertainty. GRRAS sets no numeric minimum. — [GRRAS record](https://researchprofiles.ku.dk/en/publications/guidelines-for-reporting-reliability-and-agreement-studies-grras--4/); [GRRAS checklist](https://sites.usp.br/praticaempesquisa/wp-content/uploads/sites/1049/2021/11/GRRAS-checklist.doc)
- In a radiology review, only 9 of 280 agreement/reliability studies (3.2%) justified their sample size. — [RSNA 2014 abstract](https://archive.rsna.org/2014/14005889.html)
- Systematic reviews of markerless versus marker-based motion capture use COSMIN for risk of bias. They find that agreement varies by joint, plane, and task (good in the sagittal plane, poor in transverse/frontal planes and at the ankle), and they call for standardised protocols with more participants. — [Scataglini et al. 2024, Sensors](https://consensus.app/papers/details/b2ea274d06605ac19019c898cf994898/?utm_source=claude_code); [Chougule et al. 2026, Sensors](https://consensus.app/papers/details/fa07e0dbcc25529e9b23745e1a35a345/?utm_source=claude_code)

### Inferences
- Agreement in this field is known to depend on task, joint, and plane (Scataglini 2024; Chougule 2026). That is direct evidence that a single sign's elbow-flexion agreement does not transfer to other signs, joints, or signers. COSMIN-style review would rate a one-participant, one-trial validation as very high risk of bias for generalisable measurement-error claims.
- n-of-1 designs in clinical trials gain generalisability by replicating within a subject (many crossover periods) and aggregating across subjects. The analogue here is many takes, signs, and signers. The single-take analysis is best positioned as one unit in a future hierarchical (takes within signs within signers) agreement study, for example a Bland–Altman analysis with repeated measures, or Choudhary-style mixed-model TDI. [Bland–Altman repeated-measures method is background knowledge, not verified this session]

### What can and cannot be claimed from n = 1 (synthesis for the report writer)
- **Can claim (legitimate, if stated precisely):** "For this recorded performance, under the stated and externally estimated video measurement-error model, the RMS (mean; maximum) difference between suit and video elbow-flexion angles is below ±δ, with the probability of a false acceptance at most α under that model." This is a conformity-assessment / verification statement about one item, in the sense of JCGM 101/106, and the user's simulation documents its operating characteristics.
- **Cannot claim:** that the video system agrees with the suit within ±δ for other performances, signs, signers, joints, camera placements, or lighting; any population limits of agreement, TDI, or CCC; or COSMIN/GRRAS-standard measurement error or reliability. Frame-level autocorrelated data cannot substitute for independent replicates (Q1). Augmentation replicates sample only the modelled noise (Q2).
- **Key assumption to disclose:** the claim is only as valid as the error model. Suit error, synchronisation, 2D-to-3D/depth ambiguity, and occlusion-driven systematic error need to be either included in the Monte Carlo or explicitly argued negligible.
- **Accepted alternatives for a general claim:** multi-take, multi-signer designs analysed with functional TOST or simultaneous bands across curves (Fogarty & Small 2014; Dette & Kokot 2021), or TDI/CP with appropriate tests (Choudhary & Nagaraja 2005) and repeated-measures agreement models. Sample size should be justified per GRRAS item 6, and SPM1d power tables are a starting point (Luciano et al. 2021).

- COSMIN's full user manual wording on single-participant designs was not retrieved.
- No specific guideline statement was found that explicitly prohibits n = 1. The guidelines simply make participant count and justification central.
